Know your numbers

What should you enter?

You don't need perfect numbers. Reasonable estimates are enough to create a useful starting point.

Desired annual take-home income

Think about the amount you want available to pay yourself and support your personal life. Enter this as an annual amount.

Example: If you want to take home $60,000 per year, enter $60,000.

Working weeks per year

You probably won't work all 52 weeks of the year. Subtract holidays, vacations, sick days, and other planned breaks from your expected working year.

Example: 48 weeks gives you roughly four weeks away from work.

Hours worked per week

Include all the time you spend working on your freelance business, not just the time spent with clients.

Example: A freelancer might work 40 hours per week but only bill clients for a portion of those hours.

Billable working hours

This is one of the most important numbers in the calculator. It represents how much of your total working time can actually be charged to clients.

Example: If you work 40 hours but can invoice about 28 of them, your billable percentage is 70%.

Annual business expenses

Include recurring and expected costs such as software, equipment, insurance, accounting, advertising, education, coworking, and other business expenses.

Estimated tax rate

Use an estimated effective tax rate rather than your highest marginal tax bracket. If you're unsure, use a conservative planning estimate and update it later.

Frequently asked questions

Freelance rate calculator FAQ

How much should I charge as a freelancer?

Your ideal rate depends on your income target, business expenses, taxes, available working time, and the percentage of your time that can actually be billed to clients. That's why a rate that works for one freelancer may be completely unsuitable for another.

What is a good billable percentage?

There is no universal number. Your billable percentage depends on how much time you spend on client work compared with sales, administration, marketing, professional development, meetings, and other non-billable activities. Using a realistic estimate is more useful than assuming every working hour can be invoiced.

Why isn't my hourly rate based on 2,080 hours?

A traditional full-time employee may use roughly 2,080 working hours as a starting point. Freelancers generally cannot bill every working hour, however. Vacation, holidays, administration, marketing, sales, and other business tasks reduce the number of hours that can actually generate revenue.

Should I include taxes in my freelance rate?

Yes. Your rate needs to generate enough revenue to cover your desired take-home income, business expenses, and estimated tax obligations. This calculator uses your estimated tax rate to work backwards from your target take-home income.

Should business expenses be included in my target revenue?

Yes. Business expenses reduce the amount of revenue that ultimately becomes available to you. Including them gives you a more realistic picture of the revenue your freelance business needs to generate.

Is this calculator tax advice?

No. This is a planning and estimation tool. Tax rules, deductions, business structures, and personal circumstances vary by location and individual situation. Consult a qualified tax professional for advice specific to you.

Can I use the result as my client-facing rate?

The calculated hourly rate is a useful financial floor, but it does not necessarily have to be your final client price. You may charge more depending on your experience, specialization, demand, value delivered, project complexity, and market positioning.

What if I don't know my exact expenses or tax rate?

Start with your best reasonable estimate. The purpose of the calculator is to help you understand the relationship between your assumptions and your required rate. You can return later and update the numbers as you learn more.
